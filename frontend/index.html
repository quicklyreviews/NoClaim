A market needs someone to be wrong. Insurance does not.

A prediction market pays you out of the pocket of whoever took the other side. That works for a question people genuinely disagree about. It fails completely for the ones you actually want cover against: nobody wants to be paid for betting that your warehouse will flood.

So the risks worth insuring are exactly the risks a market cannot price. Put an unloved question into a parimutuel pool and the honest outcome is a refund — which is the same as having no cover at all, arrived at more slowly.

Replace the counterparty with a pool. An underwriter is paid a premium for carrying risk, not for holding an opinion, so cover can exist for an event nobody wants to argue about.

The primitive underneath is not insurance

Strip the vocabulary away and this contract does one thing: hold money against a described future, read the world at a stated time, and move it or give it back. Insurance is the first product that shape makes. It is not the only one.

Every one of those is somebody's operations team today, reading a page and pressing a button, and being trusted not to press it wrongly. What makes them the same problem is that the hard part was never the money — it was reading evidence and exercising judgment, which is exactly what a deterministic chain cannot do and what an oracle can only do by being believed. Only the first card is built here. The other three are the same contract with a different trigger and a different payer, and that is the point.

Parametric, so there is no claim to file

A policy carries its own trigger, its own judging criteria and its own sources. At expiry, every validator independently fetches those sources over the real internet and runs the same prompt — and the transaction only lands if their verdicts match. There is no adjuster to appoint, no loss to assess, and no queue to join: anyone can settle an expired policy, including someone with no stake in it.

FIRED

The evidence says the insured event happened. The full sum insured is credited to the policyholder.

NOT_FIRED

The evidence says it did not. The pool keeps the premium, which is what underwriters are paid for.

UNKNOWN

Absent, partial or ambiguous evidence. The premium is refunded in full and the pool earns nothing at all.

That third column is the decision the whole contract is built around. Real insurance denies the claim and keeps the premium when the evidence is thin, which quietly rewards writing cover on things nobody can check. Here an unadjudicated risk earns the pool nothing, so the incentive runs the other way: write cover on measurements a validator can actually read, or be paid for it.

Solvency is structural, not a promise

Every policy reserves its full payout out of the pool the moment it is written. Cover the pool cannot back is refused at the till rather than discovered at settlement — the quote tells you before you pay.

Underwriters can only withdraw capital that is not reserved against a live policy. There is no admin key, no discretionary payout, and no point at which the contract can owe more than it holds.

The settlement we are proudest of paid nobody

A policy was deliberately written on something the sources could not possibly establish, and handed to the validators to see what they would do with it. An insurer with a bad incentive keeps that premium. This one gave it back.

The validators agreed on UNKNOWN, the premium went back to the policyholder, and the pool's earned premiums did not move by a single wei. That is the safety argument demonstrated on-chain rather than asserted in a README: when the evidence is not there, the contract refuses to decide instead of quietly taking the money.

The interesting part is five lines long

An Intelligent Contract is Python running in GenVM. It can reach the open internet and it can call a model — both of which are non-deterministic, and neither of which a chain is allowed to simply believe. The Equivalence Principle is how you get consensus out of them anyway, and choosing it correctly is the entire design problem.

strict_eq is the obvious choice and it is wrong here. It demands byte-identical output, which is fine for a price feed and impossible for a judgment: two honest adjudications of the same evidence will word their reasoning differently while agreeing completely on the verdict. So the comparison is narrowed to the one field that has to match for consensus to mean anything — outcome — and reasoning is left free to vary. Every validator fetches the sources itself; the transaction lands only if the verdicts agree.

The failure mode this buys you is the right one. When the evidence is thin the prompt is told to reach for UNKNOWN, because a wrong UNKNOWN costs a premium refund and a wrong FIRED pays the wrong person out of somebody else's capital.

Build on it

The contract is deployed, public and unowned. There is no admin key, no keeper and no backend — settlement is permissionless, so a policy that expires can be adjudicated by anyone, including a cron job that is not yours.

Sixteen methods, nine of them views. fund_pool and withdraw_pool are the underwriter's side; quote tells you before you pay whether the pool can back a given payout; buy_policy reserves it; settle_policy is the call anybody can make. Clone it, point the trigger at your own measurement, and the adjudication above is already done for you.

Source, tests and the deployment notes are in the repository, including the integration test that writes cover on StudioNet, waits for it to expire, and asserts on what the validators actually decided.
